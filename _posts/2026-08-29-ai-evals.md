Evals are the part of the AI lifecycle everyone skips — and the part that quietly kills the most POCs after launch.

You built a demo. It looked great. You showed it to five people and they said it was cool.

You never defined what "good" means. You never measured it systematically. You never set up the monitoring to know when it stopped being good. Then you shipped it, and six months later someone asked whether it was actually better than what you had before — and you couldn't answer.

There are two layers to this. Both matter.

**Build-time evals, before you ship.**

Accuracy — does the model produce correct outputs? Fairness — does it perform equitably across groups? Explainability — can you explain a given output? Compliance — will it pass review?

And the one most teams miss: will this actually change how people work? A technically perfect model that nobody uses has shipped nothing. If it doesn't fit the workflow, the metrics don't matter.

**Run-time observability, after you ship.**

Latency — are responses inside the SLA? Accuracy — is the model still performing the way it did in testing? Drift — has the input data shifted from what it was trained on? Resource usage — are we burning GPU faster than planned? Alerts — does anyone find out automatically when these break?

Without this layer, you're flying blind.

Why do evals get skipped? Mostly human reasons. Building the demo feels like progress; writing an eval suite feels like paperwork. "Good" is hard to define — fraud detection has precision and recall, but what's the ground truth for an LLM summary? And evals expose uncomfortable truths. The model that looked great in the demo might be 60% accurate on real data. That's an awkward conversation right before budget approval.

The eval gap is where a lot of the cost and infrastructure failures from earlier in this series actually live. You can't control cost or quality if you can't measure either.

A practical framework, by model type.

Traditional ML is mostly solved. Precision, recall, F1, AUC on a held-out test set. Drift detection on input distributions. Dashboards over time. Teams that ship traditional ML usually have this part.

LLMs and generative work are harder, and this is where most writing falls short. Approaches I've found useful:

**Human evaluation** — domain experts rate outputs on a rubric. Slow, expensive, still the gold standard.

**LLM-as-judge** — a stronger model grades a weaker one's outputs. Flawed, but it scales.

**Golden datasets** — curated input/output pairs you regression-test against on every change.

**Regression testing** — re-run previous inputs against new model versions before they reach production.

**Production sampling** — pull a percentage of live outputs on a schedule and evaluate those.

For agentic workflows, eval has to happen at the trace level, not just the final output. Did the agent pick the right tool? Call the API correctly? Loop unnecessarily? Get the intermediate steps right, even when the final answer looks fine? This is where evals and observability converge — and where most teams have nothing in place.

I've instrumented services with OpenTelemetry for traces, metrics, and logs. The same patterns apply to AI workloads — you're just observing agent execution instead of HTTP requests. The teams that connect their observability stack to model performance catch drift and degradation before customers do. The teams that don't find out from a complaint three weeks later.

Here's the question that ties it all together. Is the business better because of this?

Evals should answer that. Not "is the model accurate?" but did quality go up, cost go down, delivery speed up, error rates drop — and did the people doing the work get real leverage?

If your evals can't answer that, they're measuring the wrong thing.

Usage is not value. Evals are how you prove the difference.
